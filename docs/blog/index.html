1. Motivation

TL;DR Autoregressive models dominate in text generation but decode token by token. Diffusion language models can update whole sequences, but their denoising chains are slow. Image diffusion shows a path forward: distill many teacher steps into a few student jumps. IDLM asks whether this can work for discrete domain.

1.1 General Approaches: Autoregression vs Diffusion

Most modern language models use the autoregressive recipe behind Transformers and GPT-style models: generate one token, append it to the context, then generate the next token. This gives a clean probabilistic factorization,

\[ p(x^{1:L}) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} p(x^{(i)} \mid x^{1:i-1}). \]

This is why autoregression is so strong in text: training is stable, causal transformers scale well, and sampling decisions are easy to interpret. The price is the sampling loop. A \(L\)-token completion usually needs \(L\) sequential forward passes, because token \(i\) cannot be sampled before token \(i-1\) exists.

Diffusion language models use a different generation view. They start from a noisy sequence and repeatedly denoise it, so each denoising call can look at the whole sequence and revise many token positions together. Recent models such as D3PM, MDLM, SEDD, UDLM, and LLaDA show that this bidirectional, whole-sequence paradigm can be competitive for language. The promise is not that one denoising call magically solves generation. The promise is that the expensive sequential dimension might move from sequence length to the number of denoising rounds.

1.2 The Problem of Diffusion Inference

Despite their strong potential for high-quality generation, diffusion models retain a central practical limitation: inference is slow. A sampler starts from noise and repeatedly calls the denoising network; call \(k\) consumes the output of call \(k+1\). So even though one call can update all tokens in parallel, the outer loop may require \(50\), \(100\), \(250\), or \(1000\) forward passes before the sequence becomes clean.

The target regime Generating \(1024\) tokens in \(16\) forward passes means producing \(1024/16=64\) tokens per pass. This is exactly the kind of regime where diffusion could be useful, but only if the denoising chain can be compressed safely.

The natural acceleration idea is to replace many small denoising steps with a few large jumps. But the reverse transition used by many discrete diffusion samplers is factorized over positions:

\[ p_\theta(x_{t-1}^{1:L} \mid x_t^{1:L}) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} p_\theta(x_{t-1}^{(i)} \mid x_t^{1:L}). \]

This works well when the jump from \(x_t^{1:L}\) to \(x_{t-1}^{1:L}\) is small: most sentence structure is already present, and each token only needs a local correction. A large skip from \(x_t^{1:L}\) to \(x_{t-\Delta}^{1:L}\) asks one factorized transition to capture stronger cross-token dependencies:

\[ p_\theta(x_{t-\Delta}^{1:L} \mid x_t^{1:L}) \not\approx \prod_{i=1}^{L} p_\theta(x_{t-\Delta}^{(i)} \mid x_t^{1:L}), \qquad \Delta \gg 1. \]

This is the central tension. A composition of many small factorized transitions can still represent a rich final distribution, because later steps can repair earlier local mistakes. If we remove most of those steps, one factorized jump must suddenly coordinate agreement, syntax, semantics, and long-range dependencies. That is where naive step-skipping becomes fragile.

1.3 Distillation in Image Diffusion

Image diffusion made this tradeoff visible before language diffusion did. Models such as DDPM, score-based SDEs, and EDM showed that a noisy sample can be transformed into a high-quality image by following a reverse denoising process. In DDPM this process is a long reverse Markov chain; in score-based SDEs it can be viewed as integrating a reverse-time stochastic or deterministic dynamics; in EDM the same idea is refined through better noise parameterizations and sampler design.

The cost is that each reverse step requires another neural network evaluation. The original DDPM-style samplers commonly used very long chains, while accelerated samplers such as DDIM and Improved DDPM reduced the number of evaluations but did not remove the sequential nature of sampling. This is why image diffusion became the natural testbed for distillation: if a student can replace many small denoising moves with a few reliable jumps, we can keep most of the teacher's visual quality while paying for far fewer forward passes.

Distillation changed that story by asking a sharper question: can we keep the distribution learned by a strong teacher while reducing the number of sequential denoising calls? Progressive distillation, for example, trains a student sampler to imitate multiple teacher steps with one larger step, then repeats this compression. Consistency models learn a shortcut from points along a diffusion trajectory back to the clean sample, which makes one-step or few-step generation possible; this idea was later scaled in systems such as latent consistency models. A complementary line, distribution matching distillation, directly trains the student distribution to match the teacher-supported data distribution, rather than only copying local transitions.

The lesson from image diffusion is that acceleration is not only a faster sampler; it is a training problem. The student must take larger jumps without drifting away from the teacher's distribution. This is the motivation for IDLM: keep the parallel, bidirectional view of language diffusion, but transfer the successful distillation idea into the discrete text domain, where tokens are not continuous pixels, gradients cannot flow through token choices in the same way, and large factorized jumps can break cross-token structure.

The question of the day Can image-style distillation be transferred to diffusion language models? IDLM approaches this question through inverse distillation.

2. Inverse Distillation

Rather than only shortening a path, IDLM asks which distribution the teacher came from.

2.1 Two General Distillation Approaches

Continuous diffusion distillation is often organized into two families. The first is consistency-like distillation. A teacher provides a denoising trajectory, and the student learns to map different points on that trajectory to a consistent clean output. In plain terms, it teaches the model to skip along the path. This idea is powerful and relatively direct, and discrete-language variants such as SDTT and Duo-DCD show that trajectory-shortening can help diffusion language models too.

The second is distribution-matching distillation. Here we directly parameterize a student distribution \(p_\theta\) and train it to match the teacher-supported data distribution \(p^\star\). Image methods such as DMD do this with an auxiliary model that estimates whether the current student distribution matches the teacher distribution. This view is usually more involved, because the auxiliary model must be trained while the student changes, but the target is stronger: match the distribution, not merely a local denoising shortcut.

2.2 The Inverse Distillation View

Inverse Distillation is a distribution-matching-style distillation approach. The goal is to train a fast student distribution \(p_\theta\) so that it matches the target data distribution \(p^\star\). The central question is how to compare \(p_\theta\) with \(p^\star\) when the target is available through a pretrained diffusion model rather than through an explicit density. Inverse distillation answers this by comparing the diffusion models that would be learned from these two distributions.

First recall the ordinary, or forward, diffusion-training problem. We start with real data samples \(x_0 \sim p^\star\), corrupt them with the prescribed noising process, and train a reverse model to denoise them. In the notation below, the optimal diffusion model trained on \(p^\star\) is the teacher \(f^\star\):

\[ f^\star = \arg\min_f \mathcal{L}(f,p^\star). \]

Inverse distillation reverses this relationship. Instead of asking which diffusion model is optimal for a known distribution, we are given the already-trained teacher \(f^\star\) and ask which student distribution would make this same teacher optimal. A perfect student should therefore satisfy the inverse optimality condition

\[ f^\star = \arg\min_f \mathcal{L}(f,p_\theta). \]

This condition says more than "move samples toward the teacher." It says that if we took samples from the current student \(p_\theta\), noised them, and trained a diffusion model with the same loss, the model recovered by this training procedure should be \(f^\star\). In practice, we approximate this test with a second, learnable diffusion model \(f\), often called the fake model. This fake model is trained on the current student samples, so it represents the best diffusion model that the student distribution itself can explain.

The inverse-distillation loss then compares two denoisers on the same student distribution: the frozen teacher \(f^\star\), which encodes the target \(p^\star\), and the fake model \(f\), which is optimized for the current \(p_\theta\). Minimizing the resulting teacher-minus-fake gap encourages \(p_\theta\) to become a distribution for which the teacher is already the optimal diffusion model:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}(f^\star,p_\theta) - \min_f \mathcal{L}(f,p_\theta). \]

If the inverse problem is solved perfectly, the generator recovers the same data distribution that produced the teacher. This inverse-distillation line started from Inverse Bridge Matching Distillation, was extended by Universal Inverse Distillation, and was further clarified by RSD in relation to DMD-like objectives. In continuous domains, this gives both a theoretical uniqueness story and practical few-step generators.

2.3 From Images to Language

The discrete language setting changes the problem. Tokens are categorical, generator outputs can be non-smooth one-hot choices, and sampling an intermediate token can block gradients. A relaxed token may be differentiable, but it can also be out of distribution for a teacher trained on hard token states.

There is also a sequence-level issue. A text generator cannot just choose \(64\) tokens independently and hope the sentence is coherent. It has to coordinate topic, grammar, agreement, style, and long-range dependencies. So the goal is not simply "apply image distillation to text." The goal is to keep the distribution-matching promise of inverse distillation while making the objective valid and trainable for discrete diffusion language models. This is exactly the role of IDLM.

3. IDLM

IDLM extends inverse distillation to discrete diffusion language models.

3.1 Theoretical Guarantees

The first contribution is theoretical. A naive inverse objective in a discrete space might have bad minimizers, so IDLM proves the right uniqueness statement for the discrete diffusion losses used by SEDD, MDLM, and Duo. In the idealized setting,

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IDLM}}(\theta) \geq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta \,\Vert\, p^\star) \geq 0, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IDLM}}(\theta)=0 \iff p_\theta=p^\star . \]

The proof also gives a useful interpretation: IDLM matches full diffusion trajectories, not only noised marginals at isolated time points:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IDLM}}(\theta) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}\,\Vert\,\mathbb{P}^{\star}). \]

This distinction matters. DMD-like objectives compare noised student and teacher marginals at sampled times,

\[ \int_0^1 w(t)\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_t^\theta \,\Vert\, p_t^\star)\,dt, \]

while IDLM keeps the path-level comparison. A stop-gradient reduction can make inverse distillation look DMD-like, but it is not the same full gradient. In masked diffusion this difference is partly hidden by the \(\mathrm{SUBS}\) parameterization; in uniform diffusion it becomes important in practice.

3.2 Handling Discrete Non-Smoothness

In practice, IDLM parameterizes \(p_\theta\) with a student generator \(G_\theta\). To make gradients usable, the generator outputs points on the probability simplex instead of hard one-hot tokens during training. This keeps the token loss differentiable while still representing a distribution over vocabulary items.

Masked diffusion has a special simplification. With the \(\mathrm{SUBS}\) parameterization, revealed tokens are copied. Once a token is unmasked, later reverse steps carry it forward. Therefore the teacher and fake model agree on copied branches, and the IDLM signal survives only at masked positions. This is why IDLM-MDLM has a clean mask-only update instead of needing to backpropagate through every sampled intermediate token.

Uniform diffusion is harder because tokens can be revised repeatedly. IDLM uses the Duo relaxation to keep a differentiable path through intermediate states. This matters because uniform diffusion must learn both which token to choose and when to move between states.

3.3 Sequence-Level Generative Capacity

At the sequence level, the student distribution can be interpreted as a latent mixture. For a shared latent variable \(\varepsilon\), the conditional token distribution may factorize across positions; nevertheless, the same \(\varepsilon\) governs the entire sequence:

\[ p_\theta(x_0^{1:L}) = \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon} \left[ \prod_{i=1}^{L} \mathrm{Cat}(x_0^{(i)};G_\theta^{(i)}(\varepsilon)) \right]. \]

3.4 Loss Intuition

Training alternates between two updates. First, fit the fake model \(f\) on current student samples. Second, freeze \(f\) and update \(G_\theta\) with the teacher-minus-fake gap:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IDLM}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}(f^\star,p_\theta) - \mathcal{L}(f,p_\theta). \]

This gap has a simple attraction-repulsion meaning. Samples that the teacher explains better than the fake model are attractive: they look more like real data than like current student artifacts. Samples that the fake model explains too well are repulsive: they are easy for the student's own distribution to reproduce, but not necessarily supported by the teacher.

For MDLM, this becomes especially transparent. At a masked position, the local advantage is \(a_t=\log f^\star(m,t)-\log f(m,t)\). Gradient descent increases a token logit when that token's teacher-over-fake advantage is above the generator's current average advantage.

01

Teacher attraction

If \(f^\star\) gives a token or state a better denoising score than the fake model, the student is pushed toward it.

02

If the fake model already explains a sample too well, the sample may be a student artifact rather than teacher-supported text.

When the fake model catches up to the teacher on student samples, the relative advantage disappears.

The question is sharper: can a diffusion teacher be compressed into far fewer reverse steps while preserving the teacher's quality and diversity signals?

4.1 GSM8K

GSM8K tests conditional generation. The prompt is clamped, and the model generates the solution region. This is a useful stress test because local token quality is not enough: the sample has to preserve the reasoning path well enough to reach the right answer. Autoregressive baselines are still much stronger, but IDLM recovers diffusion-teacher accuracy with far fewer reverse steps.

5. Conclusion

IDLM turns inverse distillation into a discrete-language distillation method.

Diffusion language models offer parallel, bidirectional generation, but their long denoising chains make inference slow. Image diffusion suggests the right high-level move: distill the sampler. IDLM makes this move in the language domain by using inverse distillation, a distribution-matching objective rather than only a trajectory-shortening objective.

The result is a few-step generator with a clean target: match the teacher-supported data distribution. The theory gives a unique optimum, the practical method handles discrete non-smoothness, and the experiments show substantial step reductions on GSM8K and OpenWebText while keeping the teacher's quality signals largely intact.
